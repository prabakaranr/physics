1. What Is a Photon?

In modern physics (Quantum Electrodynamics, or QED), space is permeated everywhere by a fundamental quantum field called the electromagnetic field. A photon is the smallest possible discrete packet—or quantum excitation—of that field.

You cannot split a single photon of frequency ν into two half-photons of the same frequency. Instead, light exhibits wave-particle duality:

  • When traveling through space: it propagates as a quantum probability amplitude governed by Maxwell's wave equations—diffracting around edges, interfering with itself, and carrying oscillating electric and magnetic fields.
  • When created or absorbed: it delivers or takes energy in an all-or-nothing discrete packet at a localized spot (like a single atom, an LED junction, or a rod cell in your eye).

A dim blue laser and a bright red lamp differ in two independent ways. Each individual blue photon (450 nm, ~2.76 eV) carries more energy than a red photon (650 nm, ~1.91 eV). Brightness (intensity) simply measures how many photons arrive per second.

2. Properties Across the Electromagnetic Spectrum

Radio waves, Wi-Fi, room heat, visible colors, X-rays, and gamma rays are all the exact same particle—the photon. The only difference between a Wi-Fi photon and a visible green photon is its wavelength λ (and therefore its energy E = hc/λ).

3. How Photons Are Generated

Photons are not stored inside a bulb or battery waiting to leak out. They are created fresh at the exact instant an electric charge changes its energy state or accelerates. Whenever charged particles (usually electrons) lose energy to the electromagnetic field, a photon is born.

⚛️ 1. Atomic & Molecular Transitions

An electron in an atom or molecule drops from a higher energy orbital E₂ to a lower orbital E₁. Conservation of energy creates a single photon with exact energy hν = E₂ − E₁.
Examples: Neon signs, fluorescent tube mercury vapor, sodium streetlamps, lasers (stimulated emission).

💡 2. LEDs (Electroluminescence)

Inside a semiconductor p-n junction, voltage pushes electrons in the conduction band to recombine with missing-electron "holes" in the valence band, releasing a photon equal to the bandgap Eg. White LEDs use a blue LED (~450 nm) coated with yellow-green phosphor!

🔥 3. Thermal / Blackbody Radiation

Any object above absolute zero (0 K) has atoms and electrons violently jiggling and colliding. Those tiny random accelerations radiate a continuous spectrum of photons governed by Planck's law.
Examples: Incandescent filament (~2700 K), the Sun (~5800 K), and your own room walls (~295 K emitting ~10 µm infrared!).

📡 4. Accelerating Free Charges

When free electrons oscillate or decelerate rapidly, they shake the electromagnetic field and shed photons:
• Antennas: oscillating AC currents → radio/Wi-Fi photons.
• Bremsstrahlung: fast electrons braking near nuclei → X-rays.
• Synchrotron / Cherenkov: bent relativistic charges or charges faster than c/n in water.

💥 5. Particle Annihilation & Nuclear Decay

When an electron meets its antimatter twin (a positron), their mass converts completely into two gamma-ray photons of 511 keV each. Excited atomic nuclei also drop energy levels by emitting MeV gamma photons.

✨ 6. Chemiluminescence & Bioluminescence

Chemical reactions leave product molecules in an electronically excited state, which relaxes by emitting a visible photon without heat ("cold light").
Examples: Fireflies, glowsticks, deep-sea jellyfish.

5. What Happens When Photons Enter a Room

Imagine flipping on a 10-watt LED bulb in an ordinary 4 m × 4 m × 2.5 m bedroom. Even though photons move at nearly 300,000 kilometers per second, every photon follows a precise physical timeline inside the room:

t = 0 ns · Emission

~10¹⁹ Visible Photons Created Every Second

An average visible photon has energy ~2.2 eV (≈ 3.5 × 10⁻¹⁹ J). A 10 W LED producing ~3 W of optical light fires roughly 8.5 × 10¹⁸ visible photons per second outward in all directions. At any frozen instant in time, roughly 300–500 billion visible photons are mid-flight inside the room.

t = 0 to ~13 ns · Flight Through Air

Crossing the Room in 13 Nanoseconds

Air has refractive index n ≈ 1.00029, so photons travel at 99.97% of vacuum speed c—crossing a 4-meter room in just 13.3 nanoseconds. Over 4 meters of clean air, less than 0.01% of visible photons scatter off N₂/O₂ molecules (Rayleigh scattering, the effect that makes the sky blue over kilometers). Photons also never bounce off each other; light beams pass cleanly through one another.

t ≈ 13 ns · First Surface Impact

Four Possible Fates at Every Boundary

When a photon reaches a wall, mirror, table, window, or your skin, the local electrons respond to its electromagnetic wave in one of four ways:

  • Specular Reflection (Mirror / Polished Metal): Free conduction electrons oscillate smoothly in phase with the incoming field and re-radiate a coherent wave at the exact mirror angle (θi = θr). Roughly 90–99% survive each bounce.
  • Diffuse Reflection (Matte Painted Walls): Photons penetrate micrometers into the paint binder, scatter randomly off titanium dioxide (TiO₂) or pigment grains, and re-emerge in all directions—illuminating the room evenly.
  • Selective Absorption (Color!): A red shirt or red wall appears red because its dye molecules have electronic transitions matching blue and green photons (2.3–3.0 eV). Those photons get absorbed, while red photons (~1.9 eV) cannot excite the dye and scatter back out to your eyes!
  • Transmission & Refraction (Glass Window): Clear glass has an electronic bandgap larger than visible photon energies, so visible photons cannot excite its electrons. Instead, they couple briefly to glass polarization and emerge at c/n (with ~4% Fresnel reflection per glass surface).
t ≈ 50–500 ns · Multiple Bounces & Exponential Decay

How Many Times Does a Photon Bounce?

If walls have average reflectance (albedo) R, the probability of surviving k bounces is Rk. In a white-painted room (R ≈ 0.75), the average photon bounces only 1/(1 − R) ≈ 4 times (~53 ns lifetime). Even the luckiest 1-in-a-million photon is gone by ~48 bounces (~640 ns). In a dark charcoal room (R ≈ 0.15), most photons die on their very first impact (~13 ns)!

7. Until Where It Goes: The Ultimate Fate of Every Photon

Energy cannot be destroyed (First Law of Thermodynamics). So once a visible photon finishes bouncing around your room, where did it—and its energy—actually go? Every single photon meets one of four endings:

Final Fate Share of Room Photons What Physically Happens
1. Absorbed → Phonons (Heat) → Thermal Infrared ~95% – 99%+ (closed room) An electron absorbs the visible photon (~2 eV), then within picoseconds dumps that energy into atomic lattice vibrations called phonons (heat). The slightly warmed walls and furniture continuously radiate that energy back out as ~10 µm infrared photons.
2. Escapes Through Window → Atmosphere / Deep Space ~1% – 5% (near window) Passes through window glass (which transmits visible light while blocking ~10 µm room IR). Outside, photons either get absorbed by trees/ground/clouds, or—if aimed upward—pass through the atmosphere and travel across space at c indefinitely, redshifting as the universe expands.
3. Photochemistry & Vision (Retina / Plants) < 0.0001% A tiny fraction enters your pupil and hits a rod or cone cell, snapping a retinal molecule (11-cis → all-trans) to trigger an electrical nerve impulse to your brain. On a houseplant leaf, chlorophyll captures red/blue photons to split water and build sugars.
4. Delayed Re-emission (Fluorescence / Phosphorescence) Trace (if special materials present) Optical brighteners in white paper/clothes (fluorescence, nanoseconds) or glow-in-the-dark stickers (phosphorescence, minutes to hours) absorb high-energy blue/UV photons and later emit lower-energy visible photons.
Plot Twist: A "Dark" Room Is Still Full of Photons!
When you turn off the light switch, visible photons vanish in under 1 microsecond (~500 ns)—why darkness looks instantaneous to human eyes (which average light over ~20–50 milliseconds).

Yet the room is not empty of photons! Because the walls, ceiling, bed, and your own body sit around 295 K (22 °C), Wien's Law says they peak in thermal blackbody emission at λmax ≈ 2898 / 295 ≈ 9.8 µm (mid-infrared). Every square meter of wall radiates ~430 watts of infrared photons continuously—filling the dark room with trillions of invisible IR photons at all times. Thermal cameras see them clearly!

8. Deep Dives & Common Questions

When light reflects off a mirror, is it the "same" photon or a brand-new one?
In Quantum Electrodynamics (QED), photons are indistinguishable bosons, and reflection is best understood as elastic coherent scattering by the collective sea of conduction electrons in the metal coating. The incoming EM wave drives all those electrons to oscillate together; those oscillating electrons radiate an outgoing field whose amplitudes interfere constructively only along the mirror reflection angle (θi = θr) and cancel everywhere else. Because no net energy is left behind in the metal (for an ideal mirror), the outgoing photon has the exact same frequency and polarization relationship.
If a photon has zero rest mass, how does it have momentum (p = h/λ)?
In Einstein's full relativistic energy-momentum relation, E² = (pc)² + (m₀c²)². Newton's p = mv only works for slow, massive objects. For a massless particle (m₀ = 0), the equation simplifies cleanly to E = pc, so p = E/c = hν/c = h/λ. This tiny momentum is physically real—it exerts radiation pressure on walls (a 10 W bulb pushes gently on a room's walls with ~3.3 × 10⁻⁸ newtons!) and propels spacecraft solar sails in space.
Why does window glass let visible light out, yet block UV and room-temperature Infrared?
Matter only absorbs photons whose energy matches an allowed transition in that material:
  • Visible photons (1.8–3.1 eV): Too low in energy to jump silicon dioxide's ~9 eV electronic bandgap, and too high in energy to resonate with slow atomic vibrations—so visible light glides straight through!
  • Ultraviolet photons (> 4 eV): High enough to excite impurities and electronic states in soda-lime glass, so UV-B is largely absorbed.
  • Thermal Infrared (~10 µm, ~0.12 eV): Matches the vibrational stretching modes of Si–O chemical bonds, so ordinary window glass absorbs room heat strongly (which is also how greenhouses trap thermal IR!).
Do photons age or experience time while traveling across the universe?
In special relativity, the proper time interval along a path is dτ² = dt² − dx²/c². Because a photon moves in vacuum at dx/dt = c, its proper time is strictly dτ = 0 (a "null geodesic"). Strictly speaking, no valid inertial reference frame exists moving at c, so we cannot assign a clock to a photon—but in the limit v → c, time dilation approaches infinity: a photon traveling 13 billion light-years from an early galaxy experiences zero proper time between emission and absorption.
Can two photons ever collide with each other inside a room?
In classical Maxwell electromagnetism, the equations are completely linear, so two light beams pass right through each other without any disturbance (superposition). In QED, two photons can scatter off each other indirectly by briefly fluctuating into a virtual electron-positron loop ("light-by-light scattering"), but for visible light the cross-section is roughly 10⁻⁶⁵ cm²—meaning two flashlight beams crossing in your room will effectively never collide in the lifetime of the universe.
