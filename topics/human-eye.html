How the Human Eye Works

A simple, interactive look at how light is focused, how retina cells detect photons, and how light turns into electrical signals for your brain.

1. Focusing Light: The Cornea and Lens

The eye acts like a camera with two lenses that create a sharp, upside-down image on the retina:

  • Cornea (Front Window): Does ~70% of the focusing by bending incoming light rays strongly.
  • Pupil (Aperture): Expands in darkness to let more light in, and shrinks in bright light.
  • Crystalline Lens: Changes shape (thicker for near objects, flatter for far objects) to fine-tune focus.

👓 Nearsighted (Myopia)

The eyeball is too long. Rays focus in front of the retina. A concave (diverging) lens spreads the rays to fix it.

🔎 Farsighted (Hyperopia)

The eyeball is too short. Rays focus behind the retina. A convex (converging) lens bends the rays inward to fix it.

2. Sensation: Rods vs. Cones

The retina contains two types of light detectors:

🌑 Rods (~120 Million)

• Work in dim light & night vision.
• Very sensitive (can detect a single photon!).
• See in black, white, and shades of gray.

🎨 Cones (~6 Million)

• Work in bright daylight.
• Concentrated in the center for sharp reading vision.
• Three types detect Red, Green, and Blue colors.

3. The Molecular Switch: From Photon to Nerve Signal

Inside every rod and cone is a small molecule called retinal. When a single photon strikes it, the molecule snaps from bent to straight in less than a trillionth of a second!

This shape flip shuts ion channels in the cell, creating an electrical voltage drop that fires down the optic nerve to your brain.
